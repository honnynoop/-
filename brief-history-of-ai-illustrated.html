1. Introduction

The article opens by noting that enthusiasm around generative AI cooled somewhat after a sharp stock-market drop in early August 2024, when the combined market value of the so-called "Magnificent Seven" tech companies (Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla) fell sharply. Even so, the author expects the broader AI trend to keep advancing, while stressing that every organization still needs to evaluate whether and how AI genuinely fits its specific use cases. The piece serves as an orientation map — covering historical milestones, core conceptual distinctions, and illustrative applications — before a planned series on implementing AI projects in organizations.

2. Historical Development of AI Approaches

AI research dates back to the 1950s and has gone through repeated cycles of hype and disillusionment ("AI winters"). Key milestones highlighted in the article include:

  • 1956John McCarthy coins the term "artificial intelligence" at the Dartmouth Conference — originally more of a funding and marketing label for a new research field than a settled concept.
  • 1966Joseph Weizenbaum builds ELIZA, widely regarded as the first chatbot.
  • 1970sThe first AI winter: unmet expectations, limited computing power, and weak algorithms cause funding and interest to collapse.
  • 1980sA resurgence driven by rule-based expert systems that deliver useful results in narrow domains such as medicine.
  • 1987A second AI winter, worsened by the collapse of the market for specialized LISP machines and the high cost/unreliability of expert systems.
  • 1997IBM's Deep Blue defeats world chess champion Garry Kasparov.
  • 2000sGrowing data volumes ("big data") and GPU-driven parallel computing reignite progress.
  • 2011IBM Watson wins the quiz show Jeopardy! by understanding natural language and answering complex questions.
  • 2012AlexNet, a convolutional neural network, wins the ImageNet competition — a landmark deep-learning breakthrough.
  • 2016DeepMind's AlphaGo defeats Go champion Lee Sedol 4–1.
  • 2017AlphaGo Zero learns Go purely through self-play (no human game data) and decisively outperforms the version that beat Lee Sedol.
  • 2020OpenAI releases GPT-3, a 175-billion-parameter large language model.
  • 2021OpenAI releases DALL·E, generating images from text prompts.
  • 2022ChatGPT launches, showing the general public that deep-learning-based AI can hold near-human conversations across languages.
  • 2023GPT-4 is released, exceeding one trillion parameters with markedly improved capability and accuracy.
  • 2024Meta releases the Llama 3.x family of scalable, efficient open-source language models.
  • Aug 2024A NASDAQ correction tied to the "Magnificent Seven" sell-off prompts a more sober, risk-aware assessment of generative AI's costs and economic impact.

3. From "What Is AI?" to "How Is AI Conceptualized?"

The author argues that asking "What is AI, really?" inevitably produces many competing definitions, mirroring how varied and contested the concept of human intelligence itself is. AI spans everything from simple rule-based systems to adaptive machine-learning models, and it offers businesses both the chance to automate existing processes and the opportunity to innovate new business models. Rather than chase an essentialist definition, the article shifts to asking how AI is conceptualized in practice — i.e. through which guiding distinctions.

4. Guiding Distinctions in the Domain of AI

a. Weak vs. Strong AI

Weak (narrow) AI — specializes in specific tasks (e.g. Siri, Alexa, chess engines). It has no self-awareness and cannot generalize beyond its programmed scope.
Strong AI — a still-hypothetical form of AI capable of any intellectual task a human can perform, potentially with self-consciousness. Whether current systems could ever reach this remains an open question.

b. Connectionist (Sub-symbolic) vs. Symbolic AI

Connectionist AI — neural-network/deep-learning approaches modeled loosely on the brain (e.g. GPT-4, BERT), able to account for surrounding context in language tasks.
Symbolic AI — an older, now less prominent approach using explicit rules, logic, and formal symbols, exemplified by rule-based systems and early expert systems.

c. Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning

Machine learning — algorithms (e.g. decision trees, support vector machines) that learn patterns from data without being explicitly rule-programmed.
Deep learning — a subset of ML using multi-layer neural networks (e.g. CNNs, RNNs) to detect complex patterns in large datasets.

d. Large Language Models vs. Small Language Models

LLMs — models such as GPT-4 and BERT with billions of parameters; versatile but computationally expensive and less accessible.
SLMs — smaller models such as DistilBERT and Microsoft's Phi-3; more resource-efficient and cost-effective, making them attractive to smaller organizations.

e. Generative vs. Predictive AI

Generative AI — produces new content (text, images), as with ChatGPT and DALL·E.
Predictive AI — forecasts future outcomes from historical data, e.g. predictive text or security-threat detection.

f. Monomodal vs. Multimodal AI

Monomodal systems (e.g. GPT-3, RoBERTa, basic predictive-text engines) process a single data type such as text. Multimodal systems (e.g. GPT-4, DALL·E) combine multiple data types — text, images, audio — for richer generation or prediction. Cited real-world examples of multimodal prediction include forecasting the next line in an instructional video, disease-progression prediction from mixed media, predictive maintenance, and consumer-trend and demand forecasting. The article also notes that generative and predictive capabilities can be blended, citing Siemens' addition of generative AI to its "Senseye" predictive-maintenance product to improve usability.

g. Further AI Variants

  • Reinforcement learning — learning via reward and punishment (e.g. AlphaGo).
  • Transfer learning — adapting a pre-trained model (e.g. BERT) to a new task.
  • Federated learning — training models across decentralized data sources without central storage (e.g. Google's approach).
  • Explainable AI (XAI) — tools such as LIME that clarify what ML classifiers are doing.
  • Edge AI — running inference on-device (e.g. via TensorFlow Lite) rather than in the cloud.

h. Hybrid Solutions

Hybrid systems combine symbolic, rule-based components with connectionist machine learning to get the best of both: rules handle simple, repeatable tasks while ML handles complex, variable ones. Examples cited include Cognex VisionPro ViDi (rule-based vision plus deep learning for inspection tasks) and IBM's Watson Knowledge Studio (rule-based entity/relationship recognition combinable with ML for hybrid text-analysis models).

5. Future Prospects for AI

  • Autonomous agents — generative AI is moving beyond text generation toward agents that perform tasks and manage complex interactions autonomously (the article references a McKinsey report on this trend).
  • Wider adoption of Edge AI — enabling local, low-latency, real-time processing on devices.
  • Growth of Small Language Models — expected to prevail in many use cases where full-scale LLMs are unnecessarily costly or overpowered.
  • Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) — still speculative; forecasts range from under 10 years to 20–50 years out, and it would raise significant ethical, social, and safety challenges. A "technological singularity" is treated as speculation bordering on science fiction at this stage.

6. Summary

The article traces AI's path from McCarthy's 1956 coinage of the term to the 2024 tech-stock correction, then reframes the inquiry from "What is AI?" to "How is AI conceptualized?" via six guiding distinctions: weak vs. strong AI, connectionist vs. symbolic AI, machine learning vs. deep learning, LLMs vs. SLMs, generative vs. predictive AI, and monomodal vs. multimodal AI. Looking ahead, it points to the rise of autonomous agents, broader Edge AI adoption, growing reliance on smaller, cheaper language models, and the still-uncertain long-term prospect of AGI.
